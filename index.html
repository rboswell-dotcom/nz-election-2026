Who is governing right now?

The current government — National, ACT and NZ First

New Zealand is governed by a three-party coalition elected in October 2023. National leads the coalition with Christopher Luxon as Prime Minister. ACT's David Seymour is Deputy Prime Minister. NZ First's Winston Peters holds several senior portfolios. Together the three parties hold 68 of 123 seats — a working majority of 6 seats.

The opposition

Labour is the main opposition party, led by Chris Hipkins. The Green Party (co-led by Chlöe Swarbrick and Marama Davidson) and Te Pāti Māori (co-led by Rawiri Waititi and Debbie Ngarewa-Packer) also sit in opposition. Together they hold 55 of 123 seats. The Opportunity Party (TOP) is not currently in Parliament but is polling around 6%.

How your vote works

You get two votes on election day

Every voter gets two votes: an electorate vote for their local MP candidate, and a party vote for the party they want in Parliament. The party vote is more important for the overall result — it determines what share of seats each party receives nationally, regardless of who wins individual electorates.

What does your party vote actually do?

Your party vote determines the percentage of Parliament each party receives. If a party wins 30% of party votes, they get roughly 30% of the 120 seats (about 36 seats). Even if your local electorate candidate loses, your party vote still counts toward that party's national total.

The 5% threshold

A party must win either 5% of the party vote, or at least one electorate seat, to enter Parliament. Parties that don't clear this threshold receive no seats, even if they received hundreds of thousands of votes. This is why smaller parties often campaign hard in specific electorates — ACT in Epsom is a well-known example.

What is an overhang seat?

If a party wins more electorate seats than their party vote entitles them to, Parliament temporarily expands to accommodate them. In 2023, Te Pāti Māori won 6 electorate seats but only 3.1% of the party vote (which would entitle them to ~4 seats). This created a 2-seat overhang, giving Parliament 122 seats instead of 120. A further seat was added in December 2023 when National won the Port Waikato by-election, bringing the total to 123.

Why do parties need to form coalitions?

Because MMP produces proportional results, no single party usually wins a majority on its own. Parties must negotiate coalition agreements after the election, combining their seats to reach a majority. The current government (National 49 + ACT 11 + NZ First 8 = 68 seats) is a typical example of how this works in practice.

How coalitions work

Under MMP, no single party usually wins enough seats to govern alone. Parties must combine their seats to reach 61+ in a standard 120-seat Parliament. Here's what the current polling suggests.

Why does this matter to you? Your party vote doesn't just elect your preferred party — it also affects which combinations of parties can form a government. A party you vote for might win seats but end up in a government you didn't anticipate. Understanding the possible coalition mathematics helps you vote with the full picture.

From your vote to the law

Every policy on this site is a campaign promise, not law. Here's the process one actually has to survive to become a real, working piece of legislation.

Step 1 — Your vote decides who's in Parliament

Your party vote determines how many seats each party wins, and under MMP a single party almost never wins a majority outright. See for exactly how that works.

Step 2 — Coalition talks decide what actually gets pursued

When no party has a majority, the parties that can combine to reach 62+ seats negotiate a coalition or confidence-and-supply agreement. Individual policies get traded in that process — kept, watered down, or dropped entirely — and the resulting coalition agreement, not any single party's manifesto, becomes the government's actual work programme. See for what the current numbers suggest is possible.

Step 3 — It still has to pass through Parliament as a bill

A policy that survives coalition talks still has to be introduced as a bill, go through a select committee where the public can make submissions, be debated, and be voted on. It can be amended significantly, or fail to pass, even with government backing — particularly where a support party has agreed only to "confidence and supply" rather than a full coalition.

What's more likely to actually happen?

Not all commitments carry equal weight. Some parties label a specific policy a coalition "bottom line" — something they say they won't govern without — and this site flags those with a badge wherever they appear. A policy a government has already implemented is different again, flagged "Already delivered" rather than treated as a promise.
